Every winter from 1871 to 1903, the village of Harrow Lock lived on ice. The mill pond above the lock froze thick by late December, and for six weeks nearly every family in the village worked the harvest. Men cut the ice, boys guided the floating blocks down a channel to the icehouse, and women kept the accounts that decided who was paid and how much.

The work began with a test. The harvest master, an office held for most of those years by a carpenter named Elias Wren, walked onto the pond with an auger and drilled holes at twenty places. Cutting began only when every hole showed at least eleven inches of clear ice. Wren wrote the depths in a small green ledger that still survives in the county archive, and his entries show that in two winters, 1878 and 1889, the ice never reached eleven inches and no harvest took place at all.

Once the ice was ready, a horse drew a marker across the pond to score a grid of lines. Cutters followed with long saws and split the scored ice into blocks of about two hundred pounds. The blocks were floated to the foot of a wooden ramp, where a chain driven by a second horse hauled them up and into the icehouse. Packed in sawdust from the village mill, the ice kept through the summer with a loss of about a third.

Most of the ice did not stay in Harrow Lock. Canal boats carried it forty miles south to the city of Maddox, where it cooled dairies, hotels and the new hospital on Bell Street. A good winter filled the icehouse with roughly three thousand tons, and the money from the city paid for the village school and, in 1886, for a second lock gate.

The accounts are the most unusual part of the record. The village paid by the block, not by the day, and the count was kept by two women working apart, one at the pond and one at the icehouse door. If their tallies differed by more than ten blocks in a day, the harvest stopped the next morning until the difference was found. Wren's ledger notes only one such stop, on 9 January 1895, when a block had jammed under the ramp and was counted at the pond but never reached the house.

The trade ended quickly. In 1902 an ice plant opened in Maddox that could freeze city water with steam-driven machines, and its ice was cheaper than ice that had to travel forty miles by boat. The Harrow Lock icehouse took in its last harvest in the winter of 1903 and burned down, empty, in 1911. Only the ramp's stone footings remain, on the east bank of the pond, beside a plaque that gives the wrong date for the fire.
