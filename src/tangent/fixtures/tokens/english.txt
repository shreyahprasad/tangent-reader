The lighthouse keeper on Merrow Point kept two logbooks. The first was the official one, a ledger with ruled columns for wind, visibility, and the hours the lamp burned. The second was a school exercise book with a cracked green cover, and in it she wrote down everything the first book had no column for.

She noted the morning a seal slept on the landing stage for six hours and left without looking back. She noted the week the fog came in so thick that the foghorn seemed to sound from inside the walls. She noted that the supply boat's new skipper whistled the same four bars of a song over and over, and that she never learned the rest of it.

Nobody asked her to keep the second book. The harbour authority certainly did not. Their inspectors read the ledger twice a year, signed the bottom of each page, and complained about the cost of paraffin. They never once asked what it was like to watch the same stretch of water for eleven years.

When the light was finally automated, a technician arrived with a laptop and a toolbox and spent two days rewiring the lamp room. On his last evening he found the green book on the kitchen table and asked if he could read it. She said yes, mostly because nobody had ever asked.

He read it straight through, sitting on the steps outside while the beam swept over his head. When he gave it back he said it was the only record of the place that would still be worth reading in a hundred years. The ledger, he pointed out, would be scanned, indexed, and forgotten. The exercise book was the one that remembered anything.

She thought about that for a long time after she moved inland. Records are usually kept for the people who audit them, not for the people who lived them. The useful question is rarely how many hours the lamp burned. It is what you noticed while it was burning, and whether you can find your way back to that moment later.

Years afterwards, a local museum asked to borrow the green book for an exhibition about the coast. She lent it on one condition: that they display it open, not closed behind glass with a label describing it. A closed book, she said, is only a claim that something was once written down.
